\section{Abstract}
{
\bf
\looseness=-1
Language models are trained on text that survives filter cascades tuned on English web data. We ask what such a pipeline does to the earliest African-edited isiXhosa newspapers, \emph{Isigidimi samaXosa} (1870--1888) and \emph{Imvo Zabantsundu} (1884--1894), and how much of the damage is due to their pre-reform spelling. We pass 150-word windows through re-implementations of four standard gates (language ID, Gopher rules, C4 bad words, CCNet perplexity), alongside held-out modern isiXhosa and the English columns of the same pages. None of 5{,}000 historical isiXhosa windows survives, against 32.6\% of the English columns. Even after replacing Gopher's English stop words with isiXhosa ones, 25.6\% of modern isiXhosa survives but 0.0\% of the archive: the perplexity gate removes all of it. A spelling normaliser closes 22\% [19, 25] of the perplexity gap but leaves survival near zero; tokenisers charge isiXhosa 1.1--1.6$\times$ more tokens per letter than English but barely notice the 1880s spelling.
}

\section{Introduction} \label{Intro}
\looseness=-1
The isiXhosa Intellectual Traditions archive \citep{schoots2023archive} holds the first African-edited isiXhosa newspapers: correspondence from dozens of towns, political commentary and protest poetry. If such text cannot pass the filters that build pretraining data, language models inherit a record of the language with these voices removed. Its spelling predates modern conventions (\emph{kuti} for \emph{kuthi}; \emph{ku Magosa} for \emph{kuMagosa}), which invites a cheap but culturally loaded ``fix''. \textbf{Research question:} how much of this archive survives a standard pretraining filter cascade, how much worse is it modelled, and how much of that penalty is \emph{orthographic} (fixable by spelling normalisation) rather than lexical, register or OCR difference? \textbf{Hypotheses.} H1: the cascade keeps far less historical isiXhosa than modern isiXhosa or the English columns on the same pages. H2: the loss concentrates in rules that look language-neutral but are English- or modern-biased. H3: normalisation closes a measurable but partial share of the gap in LID confidence, perplexity, tokeniser cost and LM bits. H4: at a fixed budget, modern data helps more after normalisation.

\section{Background}
\looseness=-1
Pretraining corpora are the output of curation: language identification (LID), heuristic quality rules such as Gopher's \citep{rae2021gopher}, word blocklists \citep{raffel2020c4} and perplexity filters against a ``high-quality'' reference, usually Wikipedia \citep{wenzek2020ccnet}. Audits show these steps disproportionately remove minority-identity and dialectal English \citep{dodge2021c4}, and that low-resource web corpora are often poor to begin with \citep{kreutzer2022quality}. Subword tokenisers also split some languages into far more pieces than others \citep{rust2021tokenizer,petrov2023unfairness}.

\section{Methods}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{Data.} 3{,}608 OCR'd pages (718 editions) with the archive's language split: \emph{Isigidimi} 1.45M isiXhosa words, \emph{Imvo} 3.70M isiXhosa and 1.46M English. A hand audit of 50 random blocks found one mislabelled (2\% [0.4, 10.5]). Modern isiXhosa comes from MasakhaNEWS \citep{adelani2023masakhanews}, Vuk'uzenzele government news \citep{lastrucci2023vukuzenzele} and isiXhosa Wikipedia, split into \emph{disjoint roles} so nothing is evaluated on text it was built from: Wikipedia trains the perplexity reference (as in CCNet); the news and government documents are halved by a hash of their text into a \emph{lexicon} half (normaliser, isiXhosa stop words) and a held-out \emph{control} half (208k and 206k words). All corpora are cut into 150-word windows so length rules cannot drive differences (5{,}000 per historical corpus, 1{,}530 control); paragraphs are a robustness check. CIs are 95\% bootstraps over editions.

\textbf{Filters (A).} NLLB LID \citep{nllb2022} and GlotLID \citep{kargaran2023glotlid}, keep if the top label is the target with $p\geq0.5$ (swept 0.3--0.8); Gopher rules with published thresholds, with its English stop-word rule or, as an ablation, the eight most frequent words of the lexicon half; the LDNOOBW English list as in C4; a CCNet-style 5-gram character Kneser--Ney LM on the reference, dropping windows above its 60th percentile. English columns use a size-matched English Wikipedia reference and English stop words; gates are applied singly and in sequence.

\textbf{Normaliser (B0).} For every historical word type we generate all ``insert \emph{h}'' candidates at aspiration sites (\emph{p,t,k,c,q,x,r,ts,ty}) and pick the candidate most frequent in the lexicon half (59k types) if attested $\geq3$ times and more often than the original; split prefixes before capitalised words are joined. It rewrites 1{,}577 types (7.1\% of tokens, e.g.\ \emph{umteto$\to$umthetho}) and raises coverage by the \emph{held-out} control vocabulary from 52.2\% to 56.2\%, as on its own lexicon. Six volunteer isiXhosa speakers checked 165 types: precision 88\% [76, 94], recall 35\% [27, 44]. On pure aspiration fixes recall is 48\% [38, 59]; every miss is a word too rare in the lexicon half.

\textbf{Modelling (B1, B2).} B1: six tokenisers (XLM-R, which AfroXLMR \citep{alabi2022afroxlmr} reuses, NLLB, mT5, BLOOM, GPT-2, Qwen2.5); we report tokens per letter, which unlike fertility \citep{rust2021tokenizer} is not distorted by word-division conventions. B2: character KN LMs at a fixed 3M-character budget, $\{$raw, normalised$\}\times$ modern share $\{0,.25,.5,.75,1\}$; the budget is counted in raw characters so both spellings see the same pages. Splits are temporal per paper (last 15\% of editions test, previous 15\% validation); order (3--12) and discount (0.6--0.95) are tuned on validation, and test is used once. Metric: bits per \emph{raw} letter, which holds content fixed across spellings; H4 is the paired difference in how much modern share $f$ changes it under each spelling. The \textbf{gap decomposition} for a metric $M$ is $M(\text{raw})-M(\text{modern}) = [M(\text{raw})-M(\text{norm})] + [M(\text{norm})-M(\text{modern})]$, orthographic + residual.

\section{Results}
\looseness=-1
\textbf{H1 supported.} No historical isiXhosa window survives the cascade (0.0\% [0, 0]), against 32.6\% [31.2, 34.3] of the English columns (Fig.~\ref{fig:cascade}); paragraphs agree (0.0\% vs 52.6\%). With English stop words modern isiXhosa also fails (0.3\%), so the decisive comparison uses isiXhosa stop words: 25.6\% [22.8, 28.3] of modern isiXhosa then survives, but 0.0\% of the archive. \textbf{H2 partly supported.} LID is \emph{not} the bottleneck: it keeps 92--95\% of historical windows at every threshold from 0.3 to 0.8 (modern: 98--100\%). Gopher's English stop-word rule is a language filter, removing 96\% of all isiXhosa. With isiXhosa stop words it removes 8\% of modern but 32\% of historical windows: the list itself is modern (\emph{kwaye}; \emph{uthi}, 1880s \emph{uti}), and normalising barely changes this (31\%). The perplexity gate is where history is singled out: it removes 100\% of historical windows but 72\% of modern text (median 3.28 vs 2.53 bits/char). Bad words remove only 1.3\%, all false positives: surnames (\emph{Dick}, \emph{Cumming}), \emph{Exodus xx}, \emph{nambla} (modern \emph{namhla}, ``today''), and one slur quoted in a report contesting it.

\textbf{H3: orthography explains a minority of the gap.} Normalisation closes 22\% [19, 25] of the perplexity gap and 19\% [$-4$, 38] of the LID-confidence gap (Fig.~\ref{fig:gap}), yet raises survival by at most 0.1 points. Tokenisers spend 1.1--1.6$\times$ more tokens per letter on isiXhosa than on the English columns (e.g.\ XLM-R 0.367 vs 0.273), but 1880s and modern isiXhosa differ by under 6\% and normalisation moves this by at most 2\%: the tokeniser tax falls on the language, not on its 1880s spelling. In B2, a character LM trained on 1880s text models later editions equally well in either spelling (3.02 vs 3.01 bits/raw letter, CIs $\pm$0.03): with in-domain data, spelling costs nothing. A purely modern model pays 4.85 bits/letter on 1880s text versus 2.30 on held-out modern text; normalising the test text closes 7\% of the gap (4.68). \textbf{H4 supported but small.} At a fixed budget every modern share \emph{hurts} the 1880s test set (3.02$\to$3.35 at 75\% modern), and modern data hurts less after normalisation by 0.007, 0.013 and 0.019 bits/letter at 25, 50 and 75\% (all CIs exclude zero), rising to 0.17 for a purely modern model.

\section{Discussion}
\looseness=-1
The archive is erased twice over. First by rules that encode English as quality: a stop-word list, and a blocklist that cannot tell a surname or the word for ``today'' from obscenity, nor quoting a slur from using it. Second, and decisively, by a perplexity filter that encodes \emph{modern Wikipedia} as quality: even with every other rule made fair, period vocabulary (\emph{rulumente}, \emph{imantyi}), register and OCR noise put the whole archive in the tail. Normalisation is a real but small lever (a fifth of the perplexity gap; a lower bound, as it fixes half the aspiration errors), so ``just modernise the spelling'' would not rescue this text, and would overwrite an orthography that is itself evidence of isiXhosa print history. Models learn the old spelling easily from in-domain data: what the archive lacks is inclusion, not a spelling fix. Curators should (i) use language-appropriate quality rules, (ii) audit blocklist hits instead of dropping documents, and (iii) score curated heritage collections separately, keeping original and normalised layers in parallel and leaving reuse to the archive's custodians \citep{bird2020decolonising,bender2018datastatements}. \textbf{Limitations.} Filters are re-implementations; the control is modern news and government text; OCR noise is not separated from lexical change; the normaliser models only aspiration and prefix joining; B2's validation loss still fell slightly at order 12, so absolute bits are upper bounds (conclusions match at orders 8 and 12). Two failed designs (paragraphs vs articles; a reference LM trained on the control) inflated the gap.

\subsection{Changes to the plan}
(i) All corpora are compared on 150-word windows instead of paragraphs, because paragraphs against whole modern articles confounded Gopher's 50-word rule; paragraphs are kept as a robustness check. (ii) Modern isiXhosa is split into disjoint roles (perplexity reference, normaliser lexicon, held-out control), because a first run scored the control in-sample and built the lexicon from the perplexity reference. (iii) B1 reports tokens per letter instead of fertility, because 1880s word division distorts per-word counts. (iv) B2 adds a modern-only condition, a held-out modern test set, a raw-character budget, a wider grid (orders 3--12) and a paired test of H4. (v) The gold set was checked by six volunteer isiXhosa speakers; seven of eight 25-word sets were returned (169 words answered). (vi) The language-split audit was done by Matthew Purkiss and Sunay Master on 50 random blocks. \textbf{Dropped:} B3 (AfroXLMR pseudo-log-likelihood, a stretch goal).
